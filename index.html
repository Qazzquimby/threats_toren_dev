2016 Scam Safety

AI has made scams cheaper, more convincing and far more numerous. Fortunately a few habits beat most of them.

Which of these photos are AI generated?

All five were AI-generated for free in less than a second.

Average adults are barely better than chance at identifying AI content↗. A study of nearly 3,200 older adults found them only moderately better than a coin flip↗.

What AI can do now

  • Copy someone’s voice from a few seconds of audio↗
  • Swap a face live on a video call to look like anyone
  • Build a professional-looking online store in minutes↗
  • Write fake reviews and flawless, personalized messages in any language
  • Run fake profiles with photos, post histories and networks of fake followers

Scams are getting much worse

  • Scamming is cheaper so it scales. Instead of a person carefully building one scam site, hundreds can be generated at once, each with polished design and fake reviews.
  • The old “tells” are gone. Strange hands and garbled text used to give fakes away. Any flaw you can spot today will probably be fixed within a year or two, so don’t rely on spotting them.
  • The losses are growing. Reported Canadian fraud loss in 2025 was roughly 4x 2020↗↗.

Judge the request, not the content

People can't tell what's real by looking. Instead look at what they’re asking for. Be suspicious of anything involving moving money, paying a fee, an investment opportunity, a relative who needs cash, or access to your computer or accounts.

Five rules that beat most scams

1. If they contacted you, don’t act on it

That means calls, texts, emails, messages and ads. Hang up, then check through your own account, or a number or website you already have (such as the number on the back of your card). The phone number shown and the email “from” address can both be faked, so neither proves anything.

Calls about a “package” or “immigration” usually come from companies you have no account with. There’s nothing to check. Hang up. Real agencies don’t threaten arrest or demand payment over the phone.

Landline? Call back from a cell phone. Scammers can keep a landline connected after you hang up, play a fake dial tone, and answer when you dial the “real” number↗.

2. Your bank never needs you to move money to “keep it safe”

In “bank investigator” scams, a caller claims your account is compromised and tells you to move your money to a “safe account.” That account belongs to the scammer. Banks protect money by freezing it, never by asking you to move it. These scams are rising: about $28.6 million lost in just the first half of 2026↗. “Don’t tell anyone” is part of the script.

3. Avoid payments can’t be undone

Credit card payments can be reversed, which scammers want to avoid. If someone who contacted you asks for any of these, it’s a scam: gift cards; cryptocurrency (digital money, including “Bitcoin machines” in stores that turn your cash into crypto and send it to someone); a wire or e-transfer to a stranger; or cash handed to a courier.

4. Never give remote access or read out codes

Don’t let anyone take control of your computer or phone, and never read out a code that was texted to you.

5. Get a second opinion before sending money

Scammers demand urgency and secrecy because a calm outsider could spot the scam.

Ads are dangerous, and Facebook especially does not moderate their advertising.

Meta's own documents projected that 10% of 2024 revenue (roughly US$16 billion) was from ads for scams and banned goods↗, and estimated billions of “higher-risk” scam ads shown daily↗. One internal estimate said Meta’s platforms were involved in a third of successful scams in the US. Meta disputes how these documents portray it, calling the revenue figure rough and overly inclusive, and says it fights scams. It also covers Instagram and WhatsApp, and an internal review reportedly found scams easier to advertise on Meta than on Google↗.

  • Treat every ad (“Sponsored”) as untrusted. Don’t buy, invest or sign up from one.
  • Ads with celebrities, news-style stories, “guaranteed returns” or huge discounts are almost certainly fake.
  • If something looks interesting, search for the company yourself.
  • Clicking one scam ad can lead to seeing more↗.

Fake online stores: a different kind of scam

Most scams start with someone contacting you, so rule 1 doesn’t help here. You find the store, usually through an ad or a search. After you pay you're sent nothing or junk, and they may have your card details.

The sites are built to be thrown away: launched, advertised, then gone within weeks↗. One researcher found 75% of the scam stores analyzed used AI-generated images and text↗. Photos, designs and reviews (even reviewer photos) can all be fake, so judge the store by where it came from.

What actually works

  • Don’t buy from ads or links. Type the brand’s real address, or search for it.
  • Check how new the site is. A brand-new site selling brand-name goods is a warning. WebSafely shows a scam score including site age. Age alone isn’t proof either way↗.
  • Search the store’s name plus “reviews” or “scam.” No history or lots of complaints means leave. Ignore reviews on the store’s own site.
  • Huge discounts on everything (60% off and up) is a red flag.
  • Pay with a credit card. You can usually dispute it. Don’t pay by e-transfer or crypto.

Other scams to know

Investment scams

The costliest by far: $351 million of the $704 million lost in 2025↗. Often start with a social media ad or a friendly message. Be wary of any “can’t lose” offer.

“Grandchild in trouble” calls

The voice may be a clone. Hang up and call the person on their own saved number. Some families agree on a code word.

Romance scams

A long online relationship that turns to money. The photos and chats can be AI.

Recovery scams

After a loss, someone offers to get the money back for a fee. Real authorities never charge for that↗.

If it happens to you

  1. Call your bank or card company right away. Sometimes money can be stopped.
  2. Report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre: 1-888-495-8501 or antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca.
  3. Tell the police if you lost money or shared personal information.

Outside Canada: in the US, use ReportFraud.ftc.gov and ic3.gov, or the National Elder Fraud Hotline at 1-833-372-8311 (justice.gov/stopelderfraud). Elsewhere, search “[your country] report fraud” and use a government site.
